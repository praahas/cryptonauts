The story

In the 1830s, an American painter named Samuel Morse had a terrible experience. He was away from home painting a portrait when a letter arrived telling him his wife was ill. By the time he hurried back, she had already died and been buried. Devastated, he became obsessed with finding a way to send messages instantly across great distances.

Working with Alfred Vail, Morse invented the telegraph: an electrical wire that could carry pulses from one place to another. But how do you send words down a wire that can only carry electricity on or off? You invent a code.

Morse code represents every letter as a unique pattern of dots (short pulses, called "dits") and dashes (long pulses, called "dahs"). The letter E, the most common letter in English, is just a single dot — the shortest possible signal, so common words could be sent quickly. The first message ever sent by telegraph, in 1844, was: "What hath God wrought?"

Is Morse code cryptography?

Strictly speaking, no — Morse code isn't secret. Anyone who knows the code can read it. But it's a fascinating cousin of cryptography because it teaches the same fundamental idea: any message can be represented as a pattern of symbols, and any pattern can be decoded back into the original. That idea — that letters can be turned into numbers, and numbers into letters — sits at the heart of every cipher and every computer in the world.
