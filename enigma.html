ENIGMA MACHINE

MISSION 02 // THE MACHINE THAT CHANGED A WAR

BRIEFING.TXT

The story

By the 1920s, people realised the Caesar cipher and its cousins were far too easy to crack. Computers didn't exist yet, but cryptographers wanted something so complex that breaking it by hand would take a human lifetime — even longer.

A German engineer named Arthur Scherbius built such a thing. He called it Enigma. It looked like a typewriter with light bulbs above each key. Inside were three or four spinning wheels called rotors, each one wired so that pressing a key would send an electrical signal through them in a twisting, mind-bending path, finally lighting up a different letter on the lampboard. After every keystroke, the rotors moved — so even if you typed AAAAA, you'd get five different letters.

Germany used Enigma to encrypt military orders during World War II. They believed it was unbreakable. The number of possible settings was around 158 million million million.

How it was broken

It wasn't unbreakable. In Poland, mathematicians worked out some of its secrets in the 1930s. They shared their findings with British codebreakers at a country house called Bletchley Park, where a brilliant young mathematician named Alan Turing designed an electromechanical machine called the Bombe to test thousands of Enigma settings at once. Reading Germany's secret messages helped the Allies win the war — and historians believe it shortened the conflict by two years and saved millions of lives.

Turing's work also planted the seeds for the first computers. The phone in your pocket is a distant grandchild of his Bombe.

KEY IDEA
Enigma's secret weapon was that its substitution changed with every single keystroke. This made it dramatically harder than Caesar — but ultimately, every code has patterns, and patterns can be found.
