CAESAR CIPHER

MISSION 01 // SHIFT, SCRAMBLE, SHARE

BRIEFING.TXT

The story

Over 2,000 years ago, a Roman general named Julius Caesar wanted to send orders to his armies without his enemies being able to read them if they were intercepted. He came up with a simple but clever idea: every letter in his message would be replaced by the letter three positions later in the alphabet.

So A became D, B became E, C became F, and so on. The word "ATTACK" became "DWWDFN". Anyone who didn't know the trick would just see nonsense. Anyone who did could simply shift the letters back by three to read the original.

How it works

The Caesar cipher is what cryptographers call a substitution cipher — every letter is swapped for another according to a fixed rule. The "rule" here is the number of positions to shift. We call this number the key.

If the key is 3, you shift every letter forward by 3. To decrypt, you shift back by 3. There are 25 possible keys (a shift of 0 or 26 doesn't change the message at all). That's actually a weakness — a code-breaker could simply try all 25 and see which one produces real words.

KEY IDEA
A cipher's strength depends on how many possible keys there are. Caesar's 25 keys can be tried by hand in minutes. Modern ciphers have so many possible keys that even the fastest supercomputers can't try them all in billions of years.
