What is cryptography?

Cryptography is the art and science of writing messages that only certain people can read. The word comes from two ancient Greek words: kryptós (hidden) and graphein (to write). So cryptography literally means "hidden writing."

Imagine you wanted to pass a note to your best friend in class, but you were worried the teacher might intercept it. You might agree on a secret code beforehand — say, shifting every letter forward by three. So "HELLO" becomes "KHOOR". The teacher sees gibberish; your friend reads your message. Congratulations: you just invented the Caesar cipher, used by Julius Caesar over 2,000 years ago.

Two key ideas

Every cipher has two operations:

  • Encryption — turning a normal message (the plaintext) into a secret one (the ciphertext).
  • Decryption — turning the ciphertext back into plaintext.

To do either, you need a key — a piece of secret information shared between sender and receiver. In Caesar's case, the key is the number of letters you shift by.

Why does it matter?

Every time you log in to your email, send a message on WhatsApp, or buy something online, cryptography is silently protecting you. Banks, hospitals, governments, and even your favourite games all rely on it. Without cryptography, the modern internet would be impossible.

DID YOU KNOW?
The padlock icon you see in your browser's address bar means your connection is encrypted using cryptography descended from these very same ideas — just much, much more sophisticated.

How to use this site

Each lesson page has three parts: a short story about why the cipher exists, a clear explanation of how it works, and an interactive tool you can play with. There's no "right way" — try encrypting your name, your school's name, a joke. Try breaking codes your friends make. The more you experiment, the more you'll understand.

Suggested order: Caesar (shift) → Vigenère (keyword shifts) → Rail Fence (transposition) → Enigma (rotor complexity) → Morse (encoding) → Cicada 3301 (the meta-puzzle).
