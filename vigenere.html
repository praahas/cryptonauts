VIGENÈRE CIPHER

MISSION 02 // THE UNBREAKABLE CIPHER (FOR 300 YEARS)

BRIEFING.TXT

The story

For three centuries, a cipher existed that people believed was impossible to break. It was called le chiffrement indéchiffrable — "the unbreakable cipher." Invented by French diplomat Blaise de Vigenère in the 1500s, it was used by governments, military forces, and spies all over the world.

The Caesar cipher had a fatal flaw: there are only 25 possible keys. A code-breaker could try them all in an afternoon. But what if the key itself was a word, not a number? What if the shift changed for every single letter, but in a pattern that repeated the keyword?

That's the Vigenère idea. To encrypt "ATTACK AT DAWN" with the keyword "KEY", you'd write the keyword over and over above the plaintext:

KEY KEY KEY KEYKE
ATTACK AT DAWN

Now, instead of shifting every letter by 3 (like Caesar), each letter shifts by the position of its corresponding key letter. K is the 11th letter (shift 10), E is the 5th letter (shift 4), Y is the 25th letter (shift 24). So A + K(10) = K, T + E(4) = X, T + Y(24) = S, and so on.

The ciphertext is KXSGT GE TCTT.

To decrypt, the receiver uses the same keyword and subtracts instead of adding. Simple and elegant — but with one enormous advantage: if the keyword is long and random, there are billions and billions of possible keys, not just 25. Code-breakers would need more than a human lifetime to try them all.

The breakthrough

In the 1850s, Charles Babbage and Friedrich Kasiski independently discovered that Vigenère could be cracked after all — if you knew (or could guess) the length of the keyword, you could use frequency analysis on each shifted alphabet separately. But for 300 years, no one knew that. And if the key is truly random and kept secret, Vigenère is genuinely very strong — much harder to break than Caesar.

KEY INSIGHT
A long, random key is the beginning of modern encryption. Your HTTPS connection to any website uses a random key thousands of bits long. The idea is the same: make the key so large and random that brute-force guessing is impossible.
